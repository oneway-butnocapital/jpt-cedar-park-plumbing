Toilet Repair in Cedar Park, TX

We inspect running tanks, weak flushes, leaks at the base, and worn toilet parts.

Call (512) 818-1571 Ask a question

A tank that refills every few minutes can seem minor at first, but a week of running water sends more water through the fill valve and overflow tube than most homeowners expect. Over a month, a worn flapper can keep cycling, a weak fill valve can grow louder, and mineral buildup can slow the refill even further. We check the tank parts before a small refill problem turns into a toilet that will not flush.

Water around the toilet base changes the priority because the wax ring, closet bolts, or bowl can allow water beneath the flooring. A loose bowl can work against the flange with every use, while a slow leak can stain vinyl, swell wood, or dampen the subfloor. A quiet refill may wait for a planned repair if the floor stays dry, but base leaks, overflow, and a toilet that will not flush should not wait because water and waste can reach the floor.

Running tank and weak flush

Customers often call us because the toilet keeps running after a flush, the bowl barely clears paper, or the handle has to be held down to finish the flush. Other calls start with water appearing at the base, a tank that fills slowly, a hissing sound behind the lid, or a bowl water level that drops between uses. A failed flapper, worn fill valve, sticking trip lever, damaged flush valve seal, loose tank bolt, or leaking wax ring can produce symptoms that seem similar from outside the fixture. Those faults require different parts and different labor, so an inspection separates a simple tank repair from work involving the bowl, flange, or flooring.

Tank and bowl inspection

We begin at the tank by operating the handle, watching the chain and flapper seat, and checking the water line against the overflow tube. A dye test can expose a flapper leak in about fifteen to thirty minutes, and we measure the flush valve opening because many tanks use either a two-inch or three-inch valve. If the fill valve hisses, chatters, or fills slowly, we inspect the angle stop, supply connector, and incoming pressure with a gauge; residential supply pressure commonly falls within a forty-to-eighty-psi range. We can usually replace a flapper, fill valve, trip lever, supply connector, or tank seal during the visit, while a cracked bowl, damaged flange, specialty tank part, or floor repair may require parts ordering or a separate booking.

Shutoff valves and flange access

The age of the tank hardware affects the repair scope because older toilets may use a flush valve seal, handle, or fill valve that does not match common replacement parts. A seized angle stop can add work before we can disconnect the supply line, especially where corrosion has locked the valve stem or compression nut. Access at the base also matters: rusted closet bolts, tile around the bowl, a loose flange, or soft flooring can require the toilet to come up before we can reach the failed seal. A tank-specific part that must be ordered, or a floor and drain alteration that needs inspection sign-off, adds steps beyond a standard flapper or fill valve replacement.

Tank model and flush valve

We ask for the model number printed inside the tank or stamped on the underside of the lid when that marking is available. We also need to know which bathroom holds the toilet and whether the fixture is a standard tank toilet, a pressure-assisted unit, or a wall-hung bowl. A photo of the tank interior, flush valve opening, and water at the base can help us identify likely parts before we arrive. We ask whether the toilet is running now, whether the floor feels soft, and whether cabinets or flooring restrict access to the angle stop.
